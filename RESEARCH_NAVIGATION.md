# RESEARCH: The Art of Navigation

Primary-source notes for **Kaintuck** (see [TODO_KAINTUCK.md](TODO_KAINTUCK.md)), ingested from
scanned pages of:

> **Leland D. Baldwin, *The Keelboat Age on Western Waters*** (University of Pittsburgh Press, 1941).
> — Chapter 3, "The Art of Navigation," pp. 56–83
> — figure "Physiography of the Lower Mississippi" (p. 71)

Companion to [RESEARCH_BOATMEN.md](RESEARCH_BOATMEN.md) (crew, economy, culture) and
[RESEARCH_PIRATES.md](RESEARCH_PIRATES.md). Where this chapter is about **how a boat actually moved down
(and back up) the rivers and everything that could kill it on the way** — the per-leg hazard layer of the
game. Baldwin quotes early-1800s eyewitnesses (Cramer, Schultz, Nuttall, Brackenridge, Flint, James Hall,
Crockett, Ludlow); those are exactly the era Kaintuck is set in. Distances are pre-dam river miles and
run long compared to modern figures.

## 1. The pilot books — the period's "navigation system" (pp. 56–61)

Boatmen did not navigate from memory; they bought **printed river guides**. This is the in-world artifact
that justifies the player ever knowing what's around the next bend.

- **The *Ohio Navigator*** — first on record. Published by Hunter and Beaumont, Frankfort, KY, advertised
  **March 26, 1798**. Sold for **25 cents**. Described the Ohio in low water and flood; drawn from "the
  Journals of Gentlemen of Observation who have frequently navigated it."
- **The *Navigator*** (the "Pittsburgh Navigator") — *the* indispensable guide, "the Blunt of the western
  waters." First published Pittsburgh **1801** by **Zadok Cramer** (NJ Quaker, bookbinder/publisher at the
  "Sign of the Franklin Head"; died Pensacola **Aug 1, 1813**). Title settled by the 5th ed. (1806); price
  rose to **$1.00**. Ran **twelve editions** (last 1824). By 1814 it was an *olla-podrida* — navigation
  directions plus history, natural history, moral/religious asides, exploration accounts.
  - Intended **for low water.** Channels described by local features. Ripples (riffles), sand bars, snags,
    and rafts located, with directions to avoid them. **Islands were numbered:** 98 in the Ohio, 68 from
    the mouth of the Illinois to the mouth of the Ohio, 126 from the Ohio to New Orleans.
  - Distances "principally by the eye and running of the boat" — **not guaranteed accurate.** Cramer's
    1814 ed.: Pittsburgh→mouth of Ohio **1,132½ mi** (modern 979); mouth of Ohio→New Orleans **1,009 mi**
    (modern 973). The inflation came from the constant channel-crossings needed before the dams.
  - Cramer actively **corrected** between editions — e.g. re-surveyed the lower river after the 1811 New
    Madrid earthquake reshaped it. A rival pamphlet (a "James Smith," 1807) accused him of vagueness.
- **Cumings' *Western Pilot*** (Samuel Cumings, Cincinnati **1822**) — superior maps, more accurate,
  factored in the *Navigator*'s discontinuance two years later. Briefer, fewer of the colorful asides.

> **Game application:** the *Navigator* is the perfect diegetic frame for the whole UI — buy/carry a
> "Navigator" at Pittsburgh that unlocks hazard foreknowledge (named bars, the Falls chutes, the Grand
> Chain). "For low water" is a real lever: the guide is most useful, and most needed, at low stages.
> Numbered islands = a cheap, authentic way to label map features ("Island No. 68").

## 2. Going down vs. going up — seasons & embarkation (pp. 58–59, 66–67)

- **Best seasons to descend the Ohio:** spring (ice breakup ~mid-Feb through May/June) and autumn
  (Oct–Nov). **Best embarkation points:** Brownsville, Pittsburgh, Wheeling — choice depended on the river
  stage.
- **Load the boat evenly** so it draws the same depth all around; a low corner fills fast and sinks the
  boat sooner in an accident. When tied up for the night, keep the **whole hull afloat** — a sudden fall
  in the river could ground one end and swamp it.
- **Downstream is easy; upstream is the ordeal.** Round-trip economics (also in
  [RESEARCH_BOATMEN.md](RESEARCH_BOATMEN.md) §2):

| Trip | Down | Up | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| New Orleans ↔ Pittsburgh (~1,950 mi) | 4–6 weeks | 3–4 months | |
| New Orleans ↔ Louisville (~1,350 mi) | ~4 weeks | ~4 months | "six months round trip" |
| Louisville → New Orleans | ~1 month | — | flatboat |

> **Game application:** Kaintuck is a **downstream** trader's run (flatboat, one-way to market), so the
> brutal upstream months are mostly backstory/flavor — but they explain why a flatboat is broken up and
> sold for lumber at New Orleans rather than sailed home. Season choice at Pittsburgh (spring vs. autumn,
> high vs. low water) is a strong opening lever that should ripple through every later hazard.

## 3. Speed, and the flatboat's nature (pp. 61, 66–67)

- **Flatboats are solely downstream craft.** They drift with the current; the crew only pulls a stroke now
  and then to stay in the channel and dodge snags and bars. They **often ran at night.**
- **Speed = the current,** which = the river stage. Ohio low water as little as **1 mph**; in flood
  **~5 mph**. Nuttall drifted **80 mi in 24 hrs** in the Dec 1818 flood; a John Bower spoke of **100 mi**
  in a day as nothing unusual.
- For comparison, the upstream methods (below) made **~6 mi/day warping, ~30 mi/day sailing,** 15 mi a
  dependable average.
- Record runs of the age: barge "Cincinnati" NO→Cincinnati in **78 days** (1811); keel **"Susan Amelia"**
  ran the falls→Natchez (1,100 mi) in **14 days 5 hrs** day-and-night, 1813 — "nothing ought to induce
  such running but a case of life and death."
- **Lashing boats together** / traveling in fleets: ostensibly for speed (broader surface to the current),
  but really for **protection against Indians and pirates, and for companionship.** Flint saw a fleet of
  eight — one crew killing hogs, one laden with apples/cider/nuts/fruit, **a third a floating dram shop.**

> **Game application:** "run through the night" is a classic risk/reward decision — more miles, but snags
> and falling banks you can't see (§7). Current speed should scale with the chosen season/stage from §2.
> Traveling in a lashed fleet = a defensive option vs. the pirate hazard ([RESEARCH_PIRATES.md]) at the
> cost of pace/independence; the floating dram shop is a ready-made fleet-encounter vignette.

## 4. Upstream propulsion — oars, sails, poles, cordelle, warp, bushwhack (pp. 61–65)

Mostly **flavor/backstory** for Kaintuck (downstream game), but it's the vocabulary the crew speaks and
the labor that defines a riverman. Listed roughly easiest → most brutal.

- **Oars & sails.** Creoles reckoned the best oarsmen; a "Kentuck" best at the setting poles. On the Ohio
  the wind blew most often from the S/SW by day, letting a boat ascend **2–5 mph** under sail; the hundreds
  of bends made prolonged sailing hard.
- **Setting poles.** Poles ~20 ft, a **button at the top** for the shoulder, **iron-shod** at the foot.
  The crew lines the **running boards**, plants poles, and walks the boat forward against them while the
  **patroon** (helmsman) and **bowsmen** call it. Commands heard: *"Set poles!" — "Down on her!" — "Lift
  poles!" — "passé avant" — "tossed poles" — a "reverend set."* In use on the Ohio as early as ~1755.
- **Cordelle (towline).** Several hundred to **1,000 ft** of line from the masthead; a short **bridle**
  keeps the bow from swinging. Crew wades or **swims ashore — one man carrying the line in his teeth** —
  then drags the boat from the bank, fighting through brush, throwing the line over snags and trees,
  sometimes axing a path, guns strapped to backs against Indians. At the **Cornice Rocks (Grand Chain)**
  they walked a cliff edge 100+ ft above the water, snake-infested in warm weather. (Flint: cordelle men
  teased their parson boss by kicking the snakes down onto the deck.)
- **Warping.** Cordelling reversed, for water too soft to pole and shores too rough to cordelle. Two
  skiffs: one carries a cordelle upstream and ties it to a snag/tree, the crew hauls the boat up by
  **capstan or windlass**, while the second skiff carries the next line ahead. Most laborious of all —
  **6 mi a day was good.**
- **Bushwhacking.** For high water flowing among the trees: each man, in turn from the bow, **seizes a
  branch and walks aft,** hauling the boat by the bushes. Crews were entitled to **a rest every hour.**

> **Game application:** this is the dictionary for authentic crew banter and command-shouts during a
> hazard beat ("Set poles!" / "Lift poles!"). If a checkpoint or pirate fight ever forces a short
> *upstream* recovery (re-entering a channel, fighting an eddy, §6), warping/cordelling is the costly,
> time-eating action to surface. The cliff-edge cordelle at the Grand Chain ties straight into §5/§8.

## 5. The Falls of the Ohio at Louisville (pp. 67–71)

The single most famous obstacle on the route, and a natural set-piece checkpoint.

- A great **limestone ledge** across the river: fall of **~22 ft in 2 mi** — really *rapids*, dangerous
  **only in low water** (some voyagers went over without realizing it in high water).
- **Three chutes:**
  - **Indian Chute** (Indiana side) — the main one; rapid but smooth most of the way, though the rocks on
    either side were perilously close.
  - **Middle Chute** — passable at times, high water.
  - **Kentucky Chute** (southern side) — almost dry for part of the year, high water only.
- In low water it was best to **partly or wholly unload** before "taking the chute," then reload below.
- **The falls pilot:** the Kentucky assembly provided for one in **1797** — fee **$2**, required to keep a
  record of boats/cargoes taken through, resident at Jeffersonville, IN. A thriving business; the southern
  shore was favored, which is part of why **Louisville** (with the Bear Grass Creek anchorage, used to
  dodge Indian attack) kept its lead over New Albany and Jeffersonville.
- **James Hall's eyewitness run** (quotable): "...the mighty stream rolls on in a smooth unbroken sheet,
  increasing in velocity as you advance... a firm and skillful pilot, stationed on the deck, assumes
  command... the ear is stunned with the sound of rushing waters... The boat advances with inconceivable
  rapidity... 'takes the Chute'... and in a few moments she emerges... again in serene waters."
- **Going up the falls:** bateaux, keels, barges hauled by **cables** and crew muscle since French days;
  claimed boats even ascended under sail in high water. **1821:** the steamboat "Maysville" was being
  hauled up when **its cable broke** and it swung onto the rocks until the next rise. A canal was proposed
  early, a company incorporated **1804**, but work didn't begin until **1826**. (Letart's Rapids on the
  upper Ohio had permanent warping gear to help ascending boats.)

> **Game application:** the Falls = a marquee checkpoint decision. Hire the **$2 falls pilot** (safe, costs
> cash) vs. run it yourself (free, risk damage/wreck — worse at low water). An "unload→portage→reload"
> option trades time for safety with a real cargo-handling cost. Low/high water from §2 should set the
> difficulty. James Hall's passage is ready-made narration for the run.

## 6. The Grand Chain & Grand Tower of the Mississippi (p. 70)

About **30 mi above the mouth of the Ohio,** a series of difficulties extending ~30 mi up the Mississippi.

- The river boils against the **Devil's Bake Oven** (a rock jutting from the Illinois shore), is repulsed,
  and hurls itself at the **Grand Tower** opposite. Nearby: the **Cornice Rocks, the Devil's Anvil, the
  Devil's Backbone, the Devil's Tea Table** — names a traveler said marked "the divinity most religiously
  propitiated in these dangerous passes."
- Boatmen had to **land and cordelle their boats through the rapids** here — and **in the early period the
  Indians lay in wait to massacre and rob** them.
- **Wing dams** (built to force the current and serve riverside mills) were even more dangerous, often
  reaching far across the channel.

> **Game application:** the **Grand Tower** is already the game's boatmen-**initiation** site (per
> RESEARCH_BOATMEN §6 and recent kaintuck work — "pay a treat or get ducked"). This chapter adds its
> *physical* danger: a forced-cordelle bottleneck + ambush risk. A great place to stack a folklore beat
> (the "Devil's" names, the initiation) **on top of** a real navigation hazard and a pirate/Indian
> encounter — one memorable checkpoint doing triple duty.

## 7. The hazards themselves — what the river threw at you

This is the **core per-leg hazard table** for the game. (Kaintuck already does per-leg hazards and river
landings; this is the source menu.)

### a. Sand bars & islands (pp. 71–73)
- Bars and islands are "really the same thing in different stages of development." Water breaking over
  them = **ripples / riffles.** The **first 100 mi of the Ohio** were so full of them that immigrants often
  trekked overland to Wheeling to start lower down.
- **Quicksand** recognized by its luster and humidity even under the hottest sun. A permanent bar built
  from sand, gravel, clay, and driftwood lodged against an obstruction, growing for years; in low water it
  could become an **island** (willows, then sycamores/cottonwoods), and islands slowly **migrate
  upstream** over a century as the foot erodes and the head builds.
- **Grounding** was near-universal — "nothing unusual to ground several times a day." Rule: **all hands
  overboard to push.** (Capt. Thomas Carter, commanding the boat Louis Philippe traveled on, had a boatman
  hail the royals in the cabin — *"You kings down there! ...help us three-spots pull off this bar"* — and
  the "kings" obliged.) If pushing failed: **handspikes** to lever it, dig a channel, or cut **rollers** to
  roll it off. **Dec 1790:** a flour boat grounded above the mouth of the Ohio; a canoe went 40 mi to New
  Madrid for help (6 days), 11 days were lost, slippery-elm poles finally rolled it off — cost **>99 pounds
  Pennsylvania currency.**

### b. Eddies, whirlpools, boils, bends (pp. 73–77)
- Current hollowing the bed below islands forms **whirlpools / eddies** (some 2 mi around). At the **mouth
  of the Ohio** the two currents collide into irregular **"boils."** The worst-known eddy was at **Grand
  Gulf** (two whirlpools off a rocky bluff). Flint's boat was caught in a basin eddy near the **Chickasaw
  bluffs** and spun till the passengers were dizzy; a barge had just been broken in two there.
- Eddies/countercurrents are **useful to ascending boats** and let boats **land** at river towns like
  **Natchez** and **New Orleans** — but can be too strong: Schultz watched a fleet of **eleven flatboats**
  fail to land in the Natchez eddy and get carried upriver to try again; a shingle raft landed 5 mi below
  the city.
- **Bends:** current undercuts the **cave bank** on the outside → masses of earth and groves of trees fall
  in; sediment builds a **point/bar** on the inside. Downstream boats keep to the current; **ascending
  boats hug the point** (weakest current, firm bottom for poling/cordelling). A barge might make **390
  crossings** New Orleans→St. Louis chasing the best current, losing a ¼-mile each time.
- **Cutoffs** (the neck of a bend washes through — the old bed forsaken, the point left an island — boats
  "could not be expected to obey the helm in the rushing waters"). **Crevasses** (a levee breaks in high
  water). **Backing-up:** a tributary in freshet, or the main river, drives water *up* the other — 1810 the
  Ohio backed up the Muskingum and drew in New Orleans boats whose pilots, fogbound, didn't realize their
  mistake.

### c. Snags — planters, sawyers, sleeping sawyers, wooden islands (pp. 77–81)
- **Planters:** logs fixed in the bottom, root-end stuck, free end pointing a little downstream — "lay in
  wait like a couched spear to impale the boat." **Sawyers:** planters whose free ends bob up and down with
  the current. **Sleeping sawyers:** never break the surface — **the most dangerous** — coming up
  unexpectedly to stave in a hull. "**Snag**" covered both; the French called them **chicots.**
- Nuttall counted **100 snags in a single minute** and a crew member was thrown into the river when a
  sawyer struck; the steering oar was lost, entangled in the snag. **Wooden islands / rafts** of floating
  debris collected in the hollows of bends, a continual peril to clumsy flats and unskilled keels.
  (N.M. Ludlow's theatrical troupe was caught on one and had to perch the women and a baby on a log while
  they picked the raft apart.) An Indiana flatboat, holed and sinking, was saved when the patroon **threw
  an armful of quilts overboard** — the suction drew them into the hole and plugged it long enough to fit
  a new plank.
- **Davy Crockett** wrecked two stave-laden flatboats near the **Devil's Elbow** and barely escaped through
  a too-small side hole — "I was literally skin'd like a rabbit." The **Great Raft of the Red River**
  (formed ~1530; by 1833 >150 mi long, plants growing on it, used as a bridge) blocked settlement for years
  until Capt. Shreve began clearing it in **1833.**

### d. Storms, ice, earthquakes (pp. 81–83)
- **Storms** were the most dreaded peril — days of wind-driven swell, frantic bailing, holding to a branch.
  Storms wrecked boats at New Orleans (June 1805 flatboats sunk; 1806 four barges incl. the "Natchez
  Packet"; the **great storm of Aug 1812** destroyed half of the 70 vessels in harbor — and that same
  night a slave revolt, "happily abortive").
- **Ice** — "of all the enemies we meet with on the Ohio, the ice is the most formidable" (*Navigator*).
  The Ohio was usually blocked **8–10 weeks** each winter; on breakup the floating ice ran as far as the
  Mississippi. Choose a sheltered spot **below a point;** don't fell a tree over the boat (the iced tree
  may crush it). Heckewelder: 5 boats / 1,500 bushels of corn crushed in the ice, Dec 1792; eleven years
  later, **200 boats** passed the mouth of the Kentucky in floating ice — **some carrying frozen corpses.**
- **The New Madrid earthquakes** — first the night of **Dec 15, 1811,** felt to Pennsylvania and South
  Carolina. Temporary rapids formed, banks and trees fell in, lakes were created and drained, 2–3 islands
  vanished, and **New Madrid itself sank and was abandoned.** ~40–50 boats lost; for months the fresh snags
  were a peril — though in the end the quaking was thought to have **made navigation safer** by shaking
  loose old obstructions. (This is why Cramer re-surveyed the lower river.)

> **Game application:** §7 is the raw hazard deck for per-leg events. A clean taxonomy to draw from:
> **bars** (ground → push/lever/roller; quicksand camp-trap), **eddies/bends** (whirlpool spin, caving
> bank, missing-the-landing at Natchez), **snags** (planter strike, sleeping-sawyer hull breach — the
> quilt-plug as an emergency mini-game), and **weather/seismic** set-pieces (ice lockup, storm, the New
> Madrid quake as a dramatic late-route event for spring 1812 runs). Map each to severity by river stage
> and day/night choice (§3). "Skin'd like a rabbit" and the frozen-corpse ice flotilla are strong, grim
> flavor lines. Hazard *naming* should follow RESEARCH_BOATMEN §7's folk style (Devil's Elbow, etc.).

## 8. Losses, insurance, and the "wicked river" (p. 83)

- **Flatboat losses ran one-fifth to one-fourth of all boats built.** Marine **insurance 5–10%.** A
  traveler in spring 1802 heard **sixteen boats** had been lost between the mouth of the Ohio and New
  Orleans before March 12 — and that was before traffic reached real volume.
- An old flatboat captain's letter to his wife (quotable, period spelling): *"...the most eminent danger
  and the weather most excessive Cold... the Boats will run above 100 miles in 24 hours and the nights so
  dark that the Shores Cannot be known... his rest cannot exceed 4 hours out of 24... cold enough to freeze
  a dog to death — such with but few exceptions has been our Case since we embarked."*
- Flint, closing the chapter: he never traversed the Mississippi "without having heard of some fatal
  disaster... or having seen the red-shirted body of some boatman floating in the muddy waters of what that
  hardy class of men called the **'wicked river.'**"

> **Game application:** the 1-in-4-to-1-in-5 loss rate is the honest difficulty target — a Kaintuck run
> *should* feel survivable-but-deadly, not a milk run. **Insurance (5–10% of cargo value)** is a clean
> economic option at Pittsburgh: pay the premium to cap a total-loss disaster. "The wicked river" is the
> chapter's thesis and a good tone-setter / game-over framing.
