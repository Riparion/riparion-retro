# RESEARCH: River Pirates & the Natchez Trace Outlaws

Primary-source notes for **Kaintuck** (see [TODO_KAINTUCK.md](TODO_KAINTUCK.md)), ingested from
scanned pages of:

> **Leland D. Baldwin, *The Keelboat Age on Western Waters*** (University of Pittsburgh Press, 1941).
> — Chapter 5, "River Pirates and the Natchez Trace," pp. 116–133

Companion to [RESEARCH_BOATMEN.md](RESEARCH_BOATMEN.md) (Ch. 3–4, the boatmen). Where Baldwin quotes
or cites others — F. A. Rozier, Zadok Cramer's *Navigator*, the elder Audubon, J. C. Guild, John L.
Swaney, Thomas Nuttall, Gov. Claiborne — those are early-1800s sources, the exact era the game is set
in. This chapter is the spine of Kaintuck's **two great hazards**: river pirates in Phase 1 and Trace
bandits in Phase 2. Both phases of the game are *named after this chapter's geography.*

> ⚠️ **Sensitive source content:** like Ch. 4, this chapter is written in 1941 and quotes period
> sources. It refers to enslaved people as stolen "Negroes" (Mason's trade at Wolf Island, §6) and
> treats "Indians and renegades" as interchangeable villains. Use the *events* (pirate ruses, the
> Mason manhunt) without reproducing the racial framing; the game "simulates, doesn't moralize" but
> also doesn't launder the slur-laden idiom of the source.

## 1. Origins & the two pirate eras (pp. 116–117)

The "colorful history of early navigation … includes a long series of piracies in many respects as
thrilling, if not as heroic, as those of the Spanish Main." First distinct mention **c. 1760**,
centered on **Cottonwood Creek** and the **Grand Tower** on the Mississippi.

- At the **Grand Tower** (per F. A. Rozier), a keelboat bound *up* from New Orleans for the Illinois
  country was captured by "Indians and renegades," and **only one person — a young woman — was
  allowed to proceed** to Fort Chartres.
- During the Revolution, irregular forces led by the **Colberts** plundered (the Spanish called it
  piracy). After the war the **"Colbert Gang"** kept at it and became notorious.
- Because of the Colberts, the **Spanish governor in 1788 ordered all boats bound for St. Louis to
  sail in company.** This convoy attacked and captured the pirate stronghold at **Cottonwood Creek**
  and recovered part of the plunder — giving 1788 the Louisiana nickname **"l'année des dix bateaux"**
  (the year of the ten boats).
- That only moved the pirates' base. By **March 1794** (*Pittsburgh Gazette*) an armed force at the
  mouth of the Ohio was reportedly stopping boats. Two centers emerge: the **boat-wreckers** around
  **Cash River near Fort Massac** (lower Ohio), and the outlaws upstream at **Cave-in-Rock.**

> **Game application:** the **Grand Tower** (already flagged in RESEARCH_BOATMEN §6 as the initiation
> site) doubles as the *origin point of river piracy* — a perfect place for a first pirate scare on the
> Mississippi leg. "Sail in company" is a real, period-accurate lever: convoy with other boats (safer
> vs. pirates, but you split nothing and move at the slowest boat's pace) — ties directly to the
> Cairo-confluence event "travel with a nearby boat or risk going alone?" (TODO checkpoint 5).

## 2. The boat-wreckers — how the cons worked (pp. 117–120)

The lower-Ohio gangs' methods are a ready-made catalog of Phase-1 pirate events. They escalate from
swindle to murder:

1. **The card swindle.** Entice crews ashore for games of cards and "cheat them unmercifully."
2. **The treacherous pilot** (their signature). Offer to pilot boats through a dangerous channel, then
   **wreck them or drive them onto shoals** to be robbed. If the crew *refused* a pilot, the wreckers
   shouted false directions to ground the boat anyway.
3. **Sabotage by night.** Slip aboard a tied-up boat and **bore holes in the bottom or dig out the
   caulking** so it sinks slowly downstream.
4. **The "salvage" steal.** When the boat foundered, wreckers swarmed in skiffs to *"save"* the cargo —
   then rowed the goods up the creeks and the owner "never saw their property again."
5. **The decoy.** A gang member (man *or woman*) hails a passing boat asking for passage or to buy
   provisions; if the boat touches shore, the gang rushes it and the crew is "silenced forever by being
   sent to feed the catfish." (Used at Cave-in-Rock too, §3.)

> **Game application:** these five are practically a pirate-event deck. The decoy ("a figure on the
> bank waves you down — stop or hold the channel?") and the treacherous-pilot con map onto Kaintuck's
> existing pilot mechanic — note the irony with the *honest* Falls pilot at Louisville (TODO checkpoint
> 4): sometimes the pilot is your salvation, sometimes the trap. Night sabotage = a "post a watch
> tonight?" decision while tied up. Refusing the con isn't automatically safe (false directions →
> grounding), so player choices stay tense.

## 3. Colonel Plug — the comic boat-wrecker (pp. 118–119)

The "most famous practitioner of the boat-wrecking profession": **Colonel Fluger (or Pfluger) of New
Hampshire,** known throughout the West as **Colonel Plug.** His wife was **Pluggy.**

- Boatmen loved the *opéra bouffe* duel between Plug and his lieutenant **Nine-Eyes** over Pluggy. Each
  took a bullet, declared themselves satisfied, and made up. *"You are all grit!"* said Plug. *"And you
  waded in like a rale Kaintuck,"* complimented Nine-Eyes — then a bottle of whiskey and everlasting
  friendship. **(Note the source's own use of "Kaintuck" as the highest compliment for grit.)**
- Plug's method: dig the caulking out of a boat's bottom so it sinks, then "salvage" the cargo.
- **His comeuppance** (twice): once, playing cards with boatmen he'd robbed a year before, his whistled
  signal for his "huskies" was answered by the *boatmen's* confederates — three of his men thrown in the
  river, Plug stripped, tied to a tree, beaten "within an inch of his life," left for the mosquitoes.
  Later, while down in a boat's bottom digging out the caulking, a squall snapped the mooring and drove
  the boat into the river, where **it sank with Plug still aboard.**

> **Game application:** a named comic-villain pirate. "Colonel Plug" is a recognizable folk figure who
> can headline a lower-Ohio pirate encounter, and his hoist-by-his-own-petard death (drowned in a boat
> he was scuttling) is a satisfying way for the player — or fate — to turn the tables. The "tie him to a
> tree for the mosquitoes" beat echoes the boatmen's own rough justice in RESEARCH_BOATMEN §6.

## 4. Cave-in-Rock — the great pirate stronghold (pp. 119–122)

**The most widely known center of river piracy.** On the Illinois shore of the Ohio, **~20 miles below
Shawneetown.** A cave in a long limestone bluff: floor ~40 ft above low water, a near-perfect arch ~160
ft deep, with a ~55-ft rock shelf at the mouth — "a splendid one for spying up and down the river."

**The terrain did the trapping.** This is an ~8-mile gauntlet that "almost always" required a pilot:
- **Cave-in-Rock Island** just above the cave split the river and forced boats to **drift close to the
  cave mouth.**
- **Battery Rock** ~10 mi above (a quarter-mile bluff); below the cave: **Walker's Bar, Tower Rock, and
  Hurricane Island.**

**The relay-pilot con** (refinement of the boat-wrecker scheme, adopted after ~1806). Cramer's
*Navigator* (1806) actually recommended hiring a local pilot below the cave for "a dollar or two." The
gang exploited that:
- A man stationed at **Battery Rock** hails the boat, offers to pilot, and claims the *Navigator*'s
  pilot is "away on a visit." If refused, a second confederate at **Ford's Ferry** (~3 mi above the
  cave) makes the same offer.
- Whichever fake pilot got the helm decided — based on the cargo's value — whether to ground the boat at
  the cave or on Hurricane Island, or to let it through. If *both* fakes were rejected and the real
  pilot taken on, the boat passed safely — which kept suspicion off Cave-in-Rock.

**Successive gangs** held the cave; identities are "lost in a flood of aliases":
- **"Jim Wilson"** (possibly Mason under an alias, §5) raised a sign reading **"Wilson's Liquor Vault
  and House of Entertainment"** and ran a hostelry "infamous for its licentiousness." Up-river shippers
  noticed boats weren't reaching market and **no crew was heard from after passing Cave-in-Rock.** The
  Pittsburgh shippers posted a reward; **John Waller of Maysville and five companions** went in as
  "inside men" ahead of a punitive expedition, got taken into the band, and led the attack that
  destroyed the outlaws at the cave and Hurricane Island.

> **Game application:** Cave-in-Rock is the marquee Phase-1 pirate set-piece — a named, mappable place
> between Cincinnati/Louisville and the confluence (sits ~below Shawneetown, which RESEARCH_BOATMEN §9
> already flags as a candidate stop). The **relay-pilot con** is a great multi-stage encounter: you're
> hailed *twice* by "pilots" before the real one — trust which? Cargo value driving whether the pirates
> bother to wreck you ties piracy risk to the player's own greed (a heavy, valuable boat is a bigger
> target — mirrors the sandbar/draft trade-off). The "inside men" reward plot is an optional
> side-quest flavor.

## 5. Samuel Mason — pirate, then Trace bandit (pp. 121–124, 128–131)

**The best-known outlaw of the era** — and the bridge between the game's two phases (he worked the river
*and* the Trace). Despite "terrible repute," he had a "fine appearance, modest and unassuming." Born in
Virginia ~1750; a Revolutionary captain and Indian fighter, one of five survivors of the **Wheeling
ambush (Sept 1, 1777).**

**Career arc** (a usable timeline):
- Run out of East Tennessee by **John Sevier** for thievery → western Kentucky.
- **By 1791** leading the rogues at **Red Banks (Henderson, KY).** ~1796 Captain John Dunn drove him out
  but "paid the forfeit with his life."
- HQ opposite **Diamond Island**, then driven by regulators to **Cave-in-Rock for most of 1797**
  (possibly *as* the "Wilson" innkeeper). Recruited **James May** here.
- Left the cave for the Mississippi; per the elder **Audubon**, lived at **Wolf Island** (~25 mi below
  the mouth of the Ohio) stealing horses and enslaved people and robbing boats, with a fence network
  "from Virginia to New Orleans." **March 1800:** got a protective pass from the Spanish commandant at
  **New Madrid.**
- Shifted to the **Natchez Trace.** **Aug 14, 1801:** the famous **Colonel Joshua Baker robbery** (a
  Kentucky merchant heading home with four men, five horses, five pack-mules) ~20 mi from the site of
  Jackson, MS, by **four men with blackened faces.** Mason's son **John** was caught, whipped, and put in
  the **Natchez pillory** for it (crying "innocent!" with every blow), then broke jail.
- A Spanish officer concluded Mason had a network of fences across the Ohio and Mississippi valleys.
  "His methods included murder," but he left an impression of "good humor rather than ferocity."

**The end of Mason** (a complete dramatic arc):
- Late 1802 he posed as a settler at **Little Prairie** near New Madrid (party of six men + his
  daughter-in-law and her three children). **Jan 1803:** arrested by **Captain McCoy**, an Irish-Spanish
  officer; identity proven at New Madrid.
- Sent downriver toward New Orleans in irons. **Near Point Coupée (Mar 26),** the barge's mast broke;
  while the crew was ashore making a new one, the outlaws slipped their irons, seized guns, and **shot
  McCoy.** Mason was shot in the head but rallied, drove the Spanish off the boat, held them at bay till
  nightfall, then **escaped with his family** when reinforcements came.
- A **$1,000 reward, dead or alive,** was posted. He was killed by his own men for it (next item).

> **Game application:** Mason is the headline antagonist named in TODO checkpoints 9 & 11. This timeline
> lets him recur across *both* phases as a single through-line villain: a river-pirate rumor at
> Cave-in-Rock / Wolf Island in Phase 1, then the Trace bandit whose name is on every wanted poster in
> Phase 2. The **blackened-faces** detail, the **pillory at Natchez**, and the **"$1,000 dead or
> alive"** poster are concrete flavor. His "good humor, wants money not blood" reputation gives a robbery
> encounter a non-lethal branch (hand over the cash and live).

## 6. The Harpe Brothers — the senseless-violence villains (pp. 122–124, 130–131)

**Micajah ("Big") and Wiley ("Little"/"Redheaded") Harpe,** believed brothers, born ~1770 to a Tory
family in North Carolina; "treatment as Tories … warped their minds." Distinct from Mason: **Mason
robbed for money; the Harpes killed for its own sake** ("horrible and meaningless murders").

- Appeared at Knoxville 1797. Big Harpe had two women, Susan and Betty Roberts; Little Harpe married
  Sally Rice. Driven out for hog-stealing onto the **Wilderness Trail,** murdering as they went. All
  apprehended Dec 1798 (Stanford, KY); the **two men escaped,** the three women (each having borne a
  child in jail) released to rejoin them near Cave-in-Rock.
- Even the Cave-in-Rock outlaws "did not appreciate the Harpes' brand of humor" — the Harpes pushed two
  lovers off a 40-ft bluff (who survived) and drove a blindfolded horse-and-rider off a cliff — and
  **drove them out.**
- ~35 people died before "these monsters of the frontier." **Big Harpe was killed** (overtaken ~50 mi
  south of Henderson) and **his head stuck up in a tree.** Little Harpe escaped — to reappear in the
  Mason finale.

**Little Harpe's end, braided with Mason's (the payoff):**
- Two of Mason's own men — **James May** and **"John Setton"** — murdered Mason for the reward, **brought
  his head to Natchez** as proof, and applied to Gov. **W. C. C. Claiborne.** A bystander, **Captain
  Stump,** recognized "Setton" as **Wiley (Little) Harpe.** Boatmen who'd known Harpe identified him —
  one **John Bowman** by a scar Harpe himself had given him at Knoxville.
- Both tried at **Old Greenville (Jan–Feb 1804)** and **hanged Feb 8, 1804; their heads set on poles
  along the Natchez Trace.**

> **Game application:** the Harpes are the *terror* axis to Mason's *greed* axis — a bandit encounter
> where surrendering your cash does **not** buy safety (pure dread, useful sparingly for tone). The
> heads-on-poles detail is grim period texture for the Trace's deep-wilderness stretches (TODO
> checkpoints 9 & 11, where they're already named). The Mason-killed-by-his-own-men-who-turn-out-to-be-
> Harpe twist is a great endgame rumor the player hears at a northern Stand — the outlaw era literally
> ending on the road they're walking.

## 7. The Natchez Trace as a system — Phase 2 ground truth (pp. 124–128)

Why the walk home existed at all, and how it actually worked:

- **Three ways home** from New Orleans/Natchez: ship east to Baltimore/Philadelphia and cross the
  mountains; sign onto a **keelboat crew** going back up; or — the **commonest** — walk the **Natchez
  Trace.** A maze of woods trails and fords, **~700 miles** total (New Orleans→Natchez→Nashville),
  ~**500 miles between Natchez and Nashville.** Used by whites as early as 1763; busier as flatboat
  traffic grew. **Before 1800 there were no white settlements** for most of the distance.
- **Travel in parties of 3–20 for protection.** Mostly on foot; some bought horses/"Texas ponies" and
  rode **"whipsaw fashion"** (ride an hour, tie the horse, walk on; the next man rides it up, and so on).
  Natchez→Nashville took **15–20 days** in the early period; **30 miles was a good day.**
- **Provisions:** you carried your own — corn for the horses; hardtack, bacon, rice, corn for the men;
  an **emergency ration of parched, ground corn** (a spoonful "supposed to keep a man going for a day").
  Clothing: brown overalls, shirt, brogans — and **every man had a knife, blanket, canteen of whiskey,
  and a rifle or pistol.**
- **Hiding the money** (the core Phase-2 tension). Cash was converted to **specie and sewn into the belt
  or the seams of the clothes.** In camp, men hobbled the horses, then carried saddlebags and money
  belts *outside* the camp and **hid them in leaves, brush, or hollow logs.** A highwayman's "first
  object" was to find that hidden money; a night **watch** was always kept. Baldwin's wry note:
  **"the Kentucky boatmen usually fell an easy prey to the outlaws of the Natchez Trace."**

**Landmarks along the way** (candidate Stand/checkpoints, north from Natchez):
- **Grindstone Ford** on Bayou Pierre, 62 mi beyond Natchez — "the last civilized dwelling" before
  Nashville. (Aligns with TODO's Mount Locust / first-stand zone.)
- Ridge trail between the Yazoo and Tombigbee, "choked with fallen trees," single-file; water scarce, so
  parties camped at **abandoned Indian camps.** The **Big Town of the Chickasaw** at the head of the
  Tombigbee (abandoned peach/apple orchards nearby); scouts sent ahead in Choctaw/Chickasaw country.
- **The Tennessee River crossing** (~40 mi beyond the Big Town) — too deep to ford, so you paid the
  ferryman **James Colbert** a **dollar.** His Indian helpers were "cantankerous": arrive after bedtime
  and you waited for daylight. Colbert reputedly made **$2,000/yr from the ferry alone — implying
  ~2,000 travelers (mostly flatboatmen) a year.**
- **The roughest 75 miles, between the Tennessee and Duck rivers** — "bumpy hills, dense canebrakes, and
  swamps of soupy mud aptly termed **'hell holes,'**" plus mosquitoes. Between the Duck and the
  Cumberland the hills were "suggestive of camels' humps."
- At **Nashville** the parties broke up.
- **John L. Swaney,** the Trace mail carrier ~1796–1804, knew it best: ~3 weeks' hard riding for the
  1,100-mi round trip, carrying mail, provisions, a blanket, and a **tin trumpet.** He knew Mason well.

> **Game application:** this is the historical backbone for *every* Phase-2 mechanic already in the TODO.
> Concrete upgrades: **"whipsaw fashion"** is a real party/horse mechanic (one horse shared by several
> walkers); the **parched-corn emergency ration** is a perfect last-ditch provisions item; **sewing
> specie into your seams / hiding the money belt in a hollow log at camp** turns "hide your money or
> carry it ready?" (TODO checkpoint 9) into a concrete nightly choice that determines what a bandit can
> actually take. **James Colbert's ferry** ($1, and a forced wait if you arrive after dark) is a
> ready-made checkpoint event for the Tennessee/Duck crossing (TODO checkpoint 12). The **"hell holes"**
> name the swamp-crossing misery. **Grindstone Ford** = "last civilized dwelling" is a strong narrative
> beat before the deep wilderness. **"Kentucky boatmen were easy prey"** validates the whole premise:
> the player *is* the easy prey, and skill is in not being.

## 8. After Mason — piracy doesn't end, it disperses (pp. 131–133)

The death of Mason and the Harpes "by no means made the Natchez Trace a safe highway." The trade was too
profitable; "there were plenty of men to take his place."

- **1807, near Bayou Pierre:** fifteen robbers took **$4,000–5,000** from a large party; the victims
  overtook the outlaws but "did not have the courage to attack."
- **Stack Island ("Crow's Nest"),** in Nine-Mile Reach (SE corner of present Arkansas), a Mason haunt,
  stayed a pirate nest for years. Ended by **lynch law:** after a bad raiding winter (1808–9), seven
  wind-bound keelboat crews mounted a pre-dawn assault — **80–90 armed men** in skiffs killed several
  outlaws and captured 19; a boy and two women spared, **the men executed.**
- A counterfeiting angle: per Nuttall, the river gangs (~80 men, split between Stack Island and the
  mouth of the Arkansas) **passed counterfeit money.** Fall 1811, the Arkansas gang under **Clary** paid
  a flatboat crew in counterfeit; when the owner demanded restitution he was driven off by gunfire —
  until **eleven more flatboats** arrived and their crews wiped out Clary's gang (Clary lashed; he'd
  confessed buying **$1,800** in goods with counterfeit).
- **The New Madrid earthquake (Dec 1811)** literally swallowed Stack Island: Captain Sarpy of St. Louis
  tied up there Dec 15, found a gang waiting to rob him, dropped downstream — and that night the quake
  took "island and robbers" together. (It was island **No. 94** in Cramer's *Navigator*.)
- Lingering dread: in **1817** Ludlow's theatrical troupe armed every man and woman with a **saber**
  passing Plum Point below New Madrid; Nuttall's boat (1819) was hailed at **Little Round Island** by
  men **"mimicking distress to draw us to land, but in vain."**

> **Game application:** a deck of *later* river-pirate events that don't depend on the named villains —
> **counterfeit-money cons** (you're paid in bad coin and only find out later — a delayed-consequence
> cargo-sale event for Natchez or a resupply stop), the **"mimicking distress" decoy** (same family as
> §2's decoy, good for the lower Mississippi), and the **mass-flatboat posse** wiping out a gang (an
> optional "join the punitive raid" event). The **earthquake** is a dramatic random catastrophe for a
> late-Mississippi leg. Together these keep pirate risk alive *after* the player has "beaten" Mason —
> the threat is structural, not a single boss.
