This is period detail to ground Kaintuck's economy, hazards, and crew flavor in something real. Where
the book quotes other observers (Schultz, Collot, Michaux, Melish, Brackenridge, Schoolcraft, Flint,
Twain) those are early-1800s eyewitnesses — exactly the era the game is set in. Figures are nominal
dollars of c. 1790–1815.

## 1. Boats and what they cost (Ch. 3, p. 53)

Boats were priced **by the foot** of length, scaled by width and how much of the deck was roofed over.
A standard build "nearly always included one steering and four rowing oars." Boats covered the full
length cost slightly more than those covered one-third. Interior granary fittings, plus a pump and
cable, were sometimes included; pumps bought separately ran **$1.00–$1.50**.

| Craft | Price | Source / date |
|---|---|---|
| Canoe (used as a tender) | $3.00 each | Maj. Isaac Craig, 1790s |
| Canoe | $1.00–$3.00 each | Christian Schultz, c. 1808 |
| Pirogue | $5.00–$20.00 | Schultz, c. 1808 |
| Skiff | $5.00–$10.00 | Schultz, c. 1808 |
| Bateau | $1.00 / foot | Craig, 1790s |
| Bateau | $20.00–$50.00 | Schultz, c. 1808 |
| **Flatboat, 12 ft wide** | **~$1.00 / foot** | Craig, 1790s |
| **Flatboat, 14 ft wide** | **up to $1.50 / foot** | Craig, 1790s |
| Keelboat / barge | $2.66⅔ / foot (at Pittsburgh) | Craig |
| Keelboat / barge | $1.50 / foot (up the Monongahela) | Gen. Collot, 1796 |
| Barge | $4.00–$5.00 / foot | Schultz, c. 1808 |
| Keelboat | $2.50–$3.00 / foot | Schultz (c.1808) & John Melish (1811) |
| Rigged boats (Marietta) | $50.00 / ton | Schultz |

**Notes & color:**
- Pittsburgh prices were considered *exorbitant*. Collot in 1796 warned that boats should be bought
  **farther up the Monongahela**, where the same craft sold for $1.50/foot. (A real in-game lever:
  build cheaper upriver vs. convenience of the Pittsburgh boatyards.)
- The *Palladium* (Frankfort, KY), Sept. 29, 1804, claimed a thirty-ton barge cost **$3,000** and took
  **$200/year** to keep in repair — Baldwin flags this as "probably absurdly high."
- These estimates generally **excluded** equipment and cargo boxes, and mostly applied to small craft
  for the upper waters.
- Wilkinson's activity stimulated Kentucky River boatbuilding; a surviving contract had William Pope
  and Hugh Ross building boats for him.

> **Game application:** Pittsburgh build/load screen. A $75 flatboat (per TODO_KAINTUCK) sits right in
> the historical band — e.g. a ~60 ft × 12 ft flatboat at ~$1/ft + oars + fittings. Offer a width
> choice (12 ft vs. 14 ft → more cargo, deeper draft → more sandbar risk, already in the design) and an
> "buy upriver to save money" option.

## 2. Wages and crew economics (pp. 88–89)

Wages "differed with the circumstances of the voyage." **Food was always furnished** by the boat, in
addition to wages.

| Role / route | Pay | Source / date |
|---|---|---|
| Bateau oarsman, New Orleans → Illinois country | 60 Spanish dollars (trip) | 1765 |
| Patroon (same trip) | double the oarsmen (~120) | 1765 |
| Boatman, quartermaster service | $11 / month | Maj. Craig, 1794 |
| Master / patroon (quartermaster) | $15–$30 (a few got $30) | Craig, 1794 |
| Flatboatman, Kentucky → New Orleans | $35 (trip) | Wilkinson's ad, 1789 |
| **Boat captain, Kentucky → New Orleans** | **$100 (trip)** | Wilkinson's ad, 1789 |
| Boatman, the trip | $50 | Michaux, c. 1802 |
| Boatman, Mississippi | $25–$40 / month | Schultz, 1810 |
| Patroon, Mississippi | $60 / month | Schultz, 1810 |
| Missouri fur-trade engagé | $120–$150 / year | (plus heavy extra labor) |
| Supercargo | $240 / year | |
| Barge captain | $300 / year | |
| Crew member (annual hire) | $250 / year | |

- The **downriver trip ran about a month** (Pittsburgh/Kentucky → New Orleans). Statistics stayed
  fairly constant once steamboats arrived.
- Missouri-trade **engagés** were expected to "undertake a great many labors in addition to boating" for
  their annual wage.

> **Game application:** crew hiring at Pittsburgh and resupply checkpoints. Per-trip vs. per-month
> framing matters: more crew = more wages + more provisions eaten, but lower per-hazard risk (sandbars,
> snags, pirates, the Falls).

## 3. The three classes of boatmen (p. 85)

> "There were three classes of boatmen on the western waters: the regular bargemen and keelboatmen,
> whose only occupation was boating; the flatboatmen, who between voyages were farmers, merchants, or
> boatbuilders; and the men of the plains and mountains who divided their time between boating and
> trapping."

Fine distinctions were impossible — men moved freely between the classes.

> **Game application:** the player is a **flatboatman** (a trader between voyages), which is exactly the
> Kaintuck profile — a working man turning a profit, not a career riverman. Crew you hire could be drawn
> from any of the three classes (career keelers, off-season farmers, trapper-types), each a flavor hook.

## 4. Disease, mortality, and the boatmen's "medicine chest" (pp. 89–90)

The river was lethal by sickness as much as by accident.

- The enervating heat of the southern summer and the bracing northern spring made boatmen sick; the
  intemperance and debauchery of the life compounded it.
- A boatman's **medicine chest contained "only whiskey, salt, and pepper."**
- An 1802 eyewitness on the rivers: *"It is really pitiable to see such numbers of distressed objects."*
- At New Orleans the Spanish would admit boatmen to the hospital for the poor, but facilities were
  miserable, and there was prejudice against the Americans.
- **1802:** Congress declared rivermen should be regarded as seamen; a tax of **20 cents apiece** was
  collected at Fort Adams for use of the marine hospitals, and the government paid **75 cents a day** for
  a boatman's maintenance at the New Orleans charity hospital.
- **1805:** Natchez established a hospital, but receipts from a tax on boatmen ran far short of its
  expenditures (1817–1820). It led a hand-to-mouth existence for ~17 years, closed after the **1819
  epidemic**, and reopened the next year. In the 18 months after reopening it took **318 patients**, of
  whom **86 were of European nationalities** and **51 died.**
- The citizens of **Louisville** organized a boatmen's hospital in 1816 (incorporated by the
  legislature) to remove the stigma of insalubrity from the place.
- Mortality from disease was so high that **it was common for two out of five of a flatboat crew to
  perish.** Not until **1837** did Congress give real attention; marine hospitals were eventually built
  at New Orleans, Natchez, Napoleon, St. Louis, Paducah, Louisville, and Pittsburgh.

> **Game application:** disease as a first-class hazard, not just bandits and snags. "Whiskey, salt, and
> pepper" is a perfect flavor item for a useless medicine kit. Heat (lower Mississippi) and the summer
> sickly season can drive a health/morale curve; arriving at Natchez sick should be a real risk.

## 5. Crew culture — music, dancing, gambling (pp. 90–94)

The "happier side of river life" — strong material for ambient banter and downtime scenes
(cf. the recent `games/kaintuck: crew banter as ambient world-building` work).

**The fiddler.** James Hall describes a keelboatman known as **"Pappy"** — about fifty, gray-tinged hair
but fire in his eyes, kind and amicable, a drawling accent and a jest on his lips. All he brought aboard
were a few clothes tied in a bandanna — "first his violin, then 'Katy and his plunder.'" He'd sit
scraping the fiddle, singing catches and telling tales for hours, but worked with all his heart when
needed: if the boat grounded on a sandbar he was first into the water; a watch at night found him
wakeful, fiddling and singing the whole night through. At dances along the shore he was the chief
musician.

**On the boat.** When the boat was tied up, there was often dancing on deck to a fiddle. In the morning,
"setting off" from the night's berth, the men sang while keeping time with the oars:

> *Some rows up, but we rows down,*
> *All the way to Shawnee town,*
> *Pull away — pull away!*

**At a settler's house**, a dance might be arranged, the boatmen stamping a boisterous accompaniment:

> *Dance, boatman, dance,*
> *Dance all night till broad daylight,*
> *And go home with the gals in the morning.*

**Downtime ashore.** When work was done and rations eaten, the men told tales, went on coon hunts,
gambled, drank, wrestled, and spun yarns of "amorous experiences," far cities and rivers, "cannibal
Indians and white bears." The keelboatman's classic, with its swinging chorus:

> *The boatman is a lucky man,*
> *No one can do as the boatman can,*
> *The boatmen dance and the boatmen sing,*
> *The boatman is up to everything.*
> *Hi-O, away we go,*
> *Floating down the river on the O-hi-o.*

When they'd danced and sung themselves out and gambled away the last of their money, they spread
blankets on the deck or bank — with a smudge fire against the mosquitoes — and slept till dawn. If it
rained, they bedded down together on top of the lumpy cargo in the cabin.

**French boatmen** were considered far better singers than the Americans (per Bradbury and Brackenridge).
Their songs were *responsive* — the patroon led with the first couplet, the crew joined the chorus — the
words frivolous but the tunes a way to "keep from thinking too deeply of their situation in a hostile
country":

> *Dans mon chemin j'ai rencontré*
> *Trois cavalières bien montées,*
> *L'on, ton, laridon, danée,*
> *L'on, ton, laridon, dai.*

> **Game application:** rich seam for ambient crew banter and night-camp vignettes. A "fiddler" crew
> archetype (morale boost; works hard but is a luxury hire). Song snippets as flavor text on
> push-through-the-night / camp decisions. Gambling-away-the-purse maps directly to the Natchez
> Under-the-Hill night.

## 6. Wit, blackguarding, and river ritual (pp. 94–98)

**The horn.** Every boat carried a horn — for foggy days, and to "scare the devil and secure good luck."
Brackenridge: "the notes were all in a minor key, soft and weird." Gen. William O. Butler's poem
"The Boatman's Horn" was famous in its day:

> *O, boatman! wind that horn again,*
> *For never did the listening air*
> *Upon its lambent bosom bear*
> *So wild, so soft, so sweet a strain!*

**Initiations.** As ocean sailors held rituals at the equator, rivermen held them at **the Grand Tower
on the Mississippi** and **the mouth of the Platte on the Missouri.** A boatman passing those places for
the first time had to be **shaved or ducked** unless he "compromised the matter with a treat" (i.e.
bought a round).

**Hailing rituals & wit.** Boats hailed each other constantly. A classic exchange above **Limestone
(Maysville, KY)** — fast, rhyming, all "-stone":

> "Where you from?" — "**Redstone**."
> "What's your lading?" — "**Millstones**."
> "Who's your captain?" — "**Whetstone**."
> "Where're you bound?" — "**Limestone**."

**Blackguarding** was "the boatman's lighter relaxation" — every passing boat or person within earshot
became a target for a contest of insults. The man who silenced the other was the hero of the moment; the
loser, the butt of his comrades' jokes. Flint tells of a vanquished boatman who leveled his rifle at his
rival on shore; the man bolted for a tree in such plain terror that the boatman burst out laughing,
dropped the rifle, and declared he had "treed the game."

**Mark Twain** (later steamboat era) recalled flatboatmen rowing out to beg "Gimme a paper!" — the clerk
would throw over New Orleans newspapers, then heave bundles of religious tracts tied to shingles, noting
the "hard swearing which twelve packages of religious literature will command when impartially divided up
among twelve raftsmen's crews … is simply incredible."

**Timothy Flint** ran a crew nicknamed **"Flint's civil boat's crew"** — the men had agreed not to swear
or get drunk in his service; he was amused by their whimsicality but disgusted by their obscenity.

> **Game application:** the horn as an event/flavor item (fog navigation, "luck"). Grand Tower / mouth of
> the Platte initiation = a checkpoint micro-event ("pay a treat, or get ducked"). Boat-to-boat hailing
> and insult duels are ready-made ambient banter, and a "civil crew" vs. "rowdy crew" axis could tie into
> reputation.

## 7. River-feature nicknames (p. 97)

Boatmen named the river's hazards in vivid, jokey terms:

- **"General Hull's Left Leg"** — a peculiarly shaped sandbar.
- **"Paddy's Hen and Chickens"** — a group of islands (one large, several small).
- **"Devil's Elbow"** — a particularly bad turn in the Mississippi (appears on the map).
- **"Muscle Shoals"** — said to be named for the labor required to pass it.

> **Game application:** name in-game sandbars, bends, and island clusters with this style of folk
> nickname for period texture instead of generic "sandbar #3."

> ⚠️ **Sensitive source content:** p. 97–98 reproduces a Mike Fink dialect monologue (boasting about a
> New Orleans spree and a "yaller gal" cardsharp) that includes a racial slur repeated several times, in
> the racist idiom of the early-1800s frontier. **Do not lift this passage verbatim into the game.** It's
> useful only as evidence of boatman braggadocio and tall-tale style; paraphrase the *flavor* (Mike Fink
> swagger, picturesque exaggeration) without the slurs.

## 8. Honesty, theft, and desertion (pp. 98–99)

Reliability of the boatmen was "a moot subject," but the **desire for repeat business was an incentive
toward honesty** in handling cargo. More responsible captains (often part-owners of the boat, with a
stake in the cargo) ran tighter crews than the "harum-scarum" sort.

- **Mike Fink** and his crew were once caught broaching (tapping) whiskey barrels.
- Schoolcraft laid out a valuable mineral specimen on deck and returned to find it knocked to pieces.
- Brackenridge: a keelboat captain refused to pay a boatman's wages and locked up the provisions the men
  had bought for themselves — at New Madrid, before he "showed us his teeth and his pistols." The owners
  protested; the case went before a magistrate.
- Flatboat cargoes were often **stolen or destroyed through carelessness.**
- **Desertion:** French *engagés* (per Cuming) "never deserted their boats until their engagements were
  up"; **Americans frequently did.** On the harder Missouri trade, Chittenden says Creoles deserted too
  (less often than Americans) and would risk a thousand miles through hostile country to get back to
  St. Louis.

> **Game application:** validates the design's **crew desertion** and **reputation** hazards. Levers:
> American crew = cheaper/available but flightier; French/Creole crew = more loyal. A dishonest crew can
> broach your whiskey or pilfer cargo (cargo-value leak). Captain reputation affects both prices and crew
> loyalty.

## 9. Geography — "Map of Western Rivers"

The frontispiece map covers the whole Kaintuck route and beyond. Rivers: the **Ohio, Mississippi,
Missouri, Illinois, Wabash, Cumberland, Tennessee.** Named places relevant to the game's checkpoints:

- **Pittsburgh** (head of the Ohio) → **Marietta** → **Cincinnati** → **Louisville** (the Falls) →
  **Maysville/Limestone** → **Shawneetown** → **Cairo** (Ohio–Mississippi confluence) → **Memphis /
  Devil's Elbow** → **Natchez** → **New Orleans / Gulf of Mexico.**
- Also shown: **St. Louis** and **Grand Tower** on the Mississippi (the initiation site), **Eddyville**
  and **Nashville** on the Cumberland (the Trace's northern end).
- Scale bar ~300 miles / 300 km.

> **Game application:** confirms checkpoint geography in TODO_KAINTUCK and adds **Marietta**,
> **Shawneetown**, and **Grand Tower** as candidate minor stops / event sites. The map's place set is a
> good authenticity check for any new river checkpoints.
