# Gambling at Natchez Under-the-Hill: The Keelboat Era

The keelboat era at Natchez Under-the-Hill runs roughly from the 1780s through the 1820s — the rowdy years before steam made the river more respectable. The gambling houses there catered to boatmen, travelers, and poor whites, and fights over cheating at cards were common enough to be almost routine. Here's what they would have played:

## Faro

Faro was almost certainly the dominant game. It arrived in New Orleans from Europe in the early eighteenth century and spread up the Mississippi rapidly, played everywhere from rough waterfront dives to more established houses. Players bet on which cards the banker would draw from the deck — fast, simple, and relatively fair odds, which made it popular with players and irresistible to cheating dealers.

## Vingt-et-Un

Vingt-et-un (twenty-one, the ancestor of blackjack) was common throughout the French cultural orbit of the lower Mississippi. It entered the United States through New Orleans and was a fixture in Mississippi River gambling.

## Early Poker

Poker — or its predecessors — was also on the table. Poker emerged in the late eighteenth century from French and English card games and had spread through the Mississippi River region by the early nineteenth century. In the keelboat era it would have looked more like a bluffing/betting game with a 20-card deck than modern poker.

## Three-Card Monte

Three-card monte was popular, especially given the Spanish and Mexican influence filtering up from New Orleans. It was a quick hustle well suited to a transient crowd of boatmen.

## Chuck-a-Luck

Chuck-a-luck was a dice game — three dice in a birdcage tumbler — that required no reading skill and almost no learning, which made it natural for the rougher trade Under-the-Hill.

## Billiards

Billiards was also played, as Mississippians of the period enjoyed betting on billiards alongside more formal card games.

---

Racing bets and animal fights (cockfighting, bear baiting) rounded out the action for those who preferred their gambling on four legs or two wings rather than across a table.

The professionals of the era clearly preferred faro and vingt-et-un — games where a skilled or crooked dealer held structural advantages over a shifting crowd of boatmen who might be flush from a successful flatboat run down to New Orleans and looking to spend it before heading back upriver on foot along the Natchez Trace.
